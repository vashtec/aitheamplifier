A Claim on Thoughts

How This Was Made

I used AI extensively in research for this book, I confess I used all of them I could get my hands on. To be honest, I also used Google and Word Documents with its grammar and dictation autocorrects. And to be fully transparent, I also read books and communicated with other humans to compile the ideas in this book.

We never opened a manuscript and thought less of it because it wasn't handwritten, nor did we ever expect a disclosure that a typewriter or a word processor was involved in the creation of a publication. Same, I never saw a presenter acknowledge usage of PowerPoint, nor a graph be captioned with produced in Excel. The tool transitioned to the background the moment it stopped being unusual.

So how is it that we frequently hear from critics that a disclaimer is required for AI generated works? Well, that's the whole argument: did the human do the thinking or did the machine perform a task resembling human thought. One is additive, the other is zero-sum. If you just made a news article by asking ChatGPT to write an article about subject ABC with XYZ flavor, what's the value of that? Why not the reader do the same? That's the zero-sum kind, the kind where no new thought is brought to human consciousness, even though it may appear so to some.

There will always be a market for vintage artifacts, some authors to date use traditional typewriters for their manuscript. Someone still restores Ford Model Ts, or 1980s Porsche 911s. There is a market for that. But a book about AI, devoid of AI usage would be a contradiction in itself. The workflows, the thoughts, the philosophical points made in this book, arguing for human specification, judgments, and verification, are what produced this book. If the methods do not work, this book is the evidence against itself.

But my claim is that every argument here is mine. I typed every character in this manuscript, using a mechanical keyboard, the kind with a stroke resembling a traditional typewriter, connected to a computer. In a word processor. Not because it is better, faster, or more accurate, but because it allowed me to think for myself while writing. Then, I ran the claims with AI models for feedback, double checked studies, discovered where I was wrong, kept it visible where it occurred, with the correction immediately followed. I argue the use of AI models made assembling the material faster, my rationale sharper, and examples more tangible. That's how a sparring partner would help, improving your work, exposing my hand-waving, and giving you an honest grounding. That's a fair use of AI in my book (literally and figuratively), that's the one I argue is additive.

To check for yourself, go try this on your favorite AI model, unrestrict the token usage to what you can reasonably afford. Ask it to write a book called AI The Amplifier, give it all the titles, all subtitles, the structure, whatever scaffolding you want, add your two cents too. Let it run. I promise what comes back is a competent, well-organized, and unsurprisingly hollow book, a regression to the mean book with the flavor of the day. No mention of Commodore 128, no rationed compute, no confession where it got it wrong, no argument you feel you want to disagree with, nothing to be bothered about, just a cosmetic shell on the average of everything written about AI by the last training date.

It cannot produce this book and the reason is what I want you to surmise after reading this. Now someone two years from now comes and says, I did that, and it gave me a book similar to yours with a Commodore 128 story similar to yours. And I simply reply to that someone two years from now that it seems I finally wrote this book, published it, and contributed to the consciousness of humanity and AI, reflected back in an AI model. Or, maybe there was a leap in AI architecture not reflected in this book, or maybe I was wrong.

But this is my story for today.

Vahid Shabro. Physicist by training, engineer by habit, AI tinkerer since the Commodore days. Advocates for human-machine coexistence, mostly by refusing to let either side win.
